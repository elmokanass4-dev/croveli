Crochet loops explained: front loop, back loop, and both loops

Identify the front and back loops of a crochet stitch, understand FLO and BLO, and learn how loop placement changes texture.

Find the V at the top of your stitch

Look at the top edge of a row of single crochet. Each stitch has a V-shaped top with two strands. With the work held in its current working position, the strand closest to you is the front loop and the strand farther away is the back loop. Turned work can change which side of the fabric faces you, so follow the pattern’s instructions about right and wrong sides.

Working through both loops

Unless the pattern specifies otherwise, many basic stitches are worked under both strands of that V. Insert the hook under both top loops of the next stitch, then complete the stitch as directed. This gives you a useful baseline for comparing other loop placements.

Back loop only: BLO

For BLO, insert the hook under only the back strand of the V and complete the stitch. The unworked front loops create a visible line. Repeated rows of back-loop stitches can produce a ribbed texture, but the result depends on the stitch, yarn, and construction.

Front loop only: FLO

For FLO, insert the hook under only the strand nearest you. The unused back loops remain available and can form a line on the other side of the work. Some patterns use these spare loops to attach a later detail. Follow the pattern rather than switching between FLO and BLO accidentally.

The loop on your hook is different

The active loop sitting on the hook is not the front or back loop of the next stitch. Patterns may also refer to a third loop in certain stitches, such as half double crochet. That is a separate placement; check the pattern’s diagram before starting.

Try a small comparison swatch

Make three small samples using the same yarn, hook, and stitch count. Work one through both loops, one in BLO, and one in FLO. Label each sample and compare the raised lines and stretch. Count your stitches at the end of every row so a missed loop does not become a missed stitch.

Reference standards

For terminology and yarn labels, consult the Craft Yarn Council crochet abbreviations and yarn weight guidelines.
