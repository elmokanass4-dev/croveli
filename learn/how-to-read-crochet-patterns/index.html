How to read crochet patterns: a beginner’s guide

Learn crochet abbreviations, repeat instructions, stitch counts, and the difference between rows and rounds before starting a pattern.

Read the whole pattern first

Look for the skill level, finished measurements, yarn requirements, hook size, gauge, and terminology. Check whether the pattern uses US or UK terms: the same abbreviation can describe a different stitch. The pattern’s own abbreviation list is your first reference.

Common US crochet abbreviations

In US terminology, ch means chain, sc means single crochet, hdc means half double crochet, dc means double crochet, and sl st means slip stitch. An increase adds stitches; a decrease combines stitches. Their exact method should be specified by the designer. MR commonly means magic ring.

Read repeats one group at a time

An instruction such as (sc, inc) × 6 asks you to work one single crochet followed by an increase, then repeat that two-action group six times in total. Markers, parentheses, brackets, and asterisks can mean different things between designers, so check the key at the beginning.

Check the stitch counts

A number in brackets often tells you the expected count at the end of the row or round. For example, starting with 6 stitches and increasing in each stitch produces 12 stitches. Count before continuing. Catching a missed increase early is easier than repairing several rounds later.

Rows and rounds use different construction

Rows often turn the work at the end, while rounds travel around a shape. Rounds may be joined with a slip stitch or worked continuously in a spiral. Do not assume the turning chain counts as a stitch: the pattern should tell you. Place a marker in the first stitch when working continuous rounds.

Keep your place and ask precise questions

Tick completed rows and write down color changes or adjustments. If an instruction seems inconsistent, note the row number, the starting stitch count, and the count you reached. Check for published corrections before changing the pattern. A practice swatch is a good place to learn a new technique.

Reference standards

For terminology and yarn labels, consult the Craft Yarn Council crochet abbreviations and yarn weight guidelines.
